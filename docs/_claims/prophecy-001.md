title: Does “her offspring” predict the virgin birth?
description: A fair examination of the claim that Genesis 3:15 predicts Jesus' virgin birth.
robots: noindex, nofollow
claim_number: 1
summary: Christians have called Genesis 3:15 the first gospel. But does its reference to the woman's offspring actually predict a virgin birth?
ot_reference: Genesis 3:15
ot_text: >-
  And I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your seed and
  her seed. He will crush your head, and you will strike his heel.
ot_note: >-
  The same Hebrew verb appears twice and may be rendered “crush,” “bruise,”
  or “strike.”
christian_case: >-
  Many Christians read this as the Bible's first promise of a deliverer. The
  serpent has brought sin and death into the story, but a future offspring of
  the woman will strike its head. Some evangelical interpreters argue that the
  singular grammar and the developing “seed” theme in Genesis point toward one
  representative victor. Read alongside Matthew's account of Jesus' conception
  by the Holy Spirit and Paul's description of him as “born of a woman,” the
  unusual phrase “her seed” is then taken as a veiled anticipation of the virgin
  birth. This is a cumulative Christian reading: no single phrase carries the
  whole case.
critical_case: >-
  The narrower virgin-birth claim goes beyond what Genesis says. The verse
  contrasts the woman with the serpent and her offspring with the serpent's;
  it never says that the offspring has no human father. Genesis itself speaks
  of Hagar's offspring (16:10) and Rebekah's offspring (24:60) without implying
  miraculous conception. The Hebrew word can refer collectively to descendants,
  and even an individual reading would identify a future person—not the manner
  of his conception. Neither Galatians 4:4 nor Matthew 1:18 cites Genesis 3:15.
  Later Christian texts and Eve–Mary typology can give the verse a meaningful
  canonical role, but that is different from finding the virgin birth predicted
  in its original wording.
verdict_label: Later typology, not a virgin-birth prediction
verdict: >-
  Genesis 3:15 can support a Christian messianic or typological reading. Its
  wording does not independently predict a virgin birth; virgin conception is
  explicit in the later birth narratives, not in the phrase “her offspring.”
