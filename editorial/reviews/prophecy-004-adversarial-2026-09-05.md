## Findings

1. Do not frame the pronoun in Genesis 9:27 too cheaply. In the BSB wording, God is
   the preceding grammatical subject, even though many translations and commentators
   understand Japheth as the subject of “may he dwell.”
2. Keep the rejection of the slogan as prediction separate from the more defensible
   typological or canonical reading, which does not require explicit prediction.
3. “Later Christian theology” can sound external to the canon. Say that the decisive
   move is later relative to Genesis and depends on later canonical Christian claims.
4. Do not imply broad scholarly consensus from two historical-critical sources.
5. Strengthen the canonical case: Genesis 10–11 carries Shem's line to Abraham, and
   Luke carries the line to Jesus.
6. Luke 3:36 includes Cainan between Arphaxad and Shem, unlike the Masoretic genealogy
   in Genesis 11. This does not affect descent from Shem but is worth noting.
7. “YHWH, God of Shem” can mark election, covenantal privilege and sacred lineage,
   not merely identify a people's deity.
8. Continue distinguishing the unusually weak inherited slogan from the best
   theological construction offered in its defence.

No clear straw man was found because the record supplied the stronger God-as-subject
and incarnation reading rather than criticizing only the inherited wording.

## Strongest rebuttal

A Christian canonical reader can argue that Genesis 9 identifies Shem as the blessed
line of YHWH's special presence; Genesis 10–11 carries that line to Abraham; Luke
carries it to Jesus; and Christian doctrine identifies Jesus as God incarnate. On that
reading, Genesis 9 need not explicitly say “God will become Shem's son” to be a true
seed-form prophecy whose meaning becomes clear only in later canonical fulfilment.
The proposed verdict is strongest against the slogan as a source claim, but less
decisive against a typological fulfilment claim.
