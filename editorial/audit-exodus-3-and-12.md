# Exodus 3 and 12 shared audit (claims 021–025)

Exodus 3:12–15 is God's response to Moses's reluctance about leading Israel out of
Egypt. The promise “I will be with you” precedes the wordplay in *ehyeh asher ehyeh*
(“I am/will be who I am/will be”), and verse 15 supplies YHWH as the enduring name.
The BSB's “I AM WHO I AM” is one possible rendering, not a proof that John's Greek
*egō eimi* reproduces the name. The Greek Exodus 3:14 has *egō eimi ho ōn* and
subsequently *ho ōn* as the speaker's designation. John 8:58 has *egō eimi* without
*ho ōn*; its contrast between Abraham's coming-to-be and Jesus's “I am” is striking,
and the attempted stoning supports a high Christological reading, but Exodus 3 was
not a prediction of a future person. John 4:25–26 answers the Samaritan woman's
question about Messiah (“I am he”), not a citation of the divine name.

Exodus 12 sets out a historical and continuing ritual to remember Israel's escape
from Egypt. The chosen animal may be from sheep **or goats** (12:5); “without blemish”
is also a general sacrificial requirement. Blood is put on household doorframes as
a sign in the firstborn-plague narrative (12:7, 13, 21–23), not used there to
remove sins. Families repeat the rite and explain its meaning to children
(12:24–27). Eating it within one house and leaving its bones unbroken (12:46) are
rules for handling that meal. These are not, in their Exodus setting, descriptions
of a future execution or atonement.

The later Christian reception is substantial rather than accidental. 1 Corinthians
5:7 explicitly names Christ “our Passover lamb”; 1 Peter 1:19 describes his blood
with sacrificial lamb language, though whether that verse singles out Passover is
debated. Romans 5:8 says Christ died for sinners, and **verse 9** adds salvation
from wrath through his blood; neither identifies Exodus 12. John 19:31–36 describes
Jesus's legs left unbroken and explicitly says Scripture is fulfilled. The source
of its quotation is debated among Exodus 12:46, Numbers 9:12 (another Passover
rule), and Psalm 34:20 (protection of the righteous sufferer). Passover framing
makes the Exodus/Numbers reading strong; source ambiguity limits any claim of a
single, unambiguous Exodus citation. The intended Christian typology is coherent,
but prediction and later theological application must be evaluated separately.
