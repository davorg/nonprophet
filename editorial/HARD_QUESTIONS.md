# Editorial decisions

These decisions govern the audit. Routine reference corrections and individual
evidence assessments do not appear here.

## HQ-001: What counts as a “prophecy” for inclusion?

**Decision: accepted.**

The seed list combines at least five different things:

1. an apparent prediction later applied to Jesus;
2. an OT passage explicitly quoted or applied to Jesus by an NT author;
3. typology explicitly constructed by an NT author;
4. later Christian typology not stated in the NT; and
5. a loose resemblance or shared theological theme.

Examples of category 4 or 5 appear immediately: Enoch’s translation (entry 3),
Melchizedek’s bread and wine as the Last Supper (entry 9), and Isaac’s ram as
John’s “Lamb of God” (entry 11).

Retain all claims Christians actually circulate, but label
the claim type prominently. Do not let the database itself imply that every item was
a predictive oracle.

## HQ-002: Is the publishing unit a claim or an OT passage?

**Decision: publish individual claims.**

There are 351 claims but only 314 distinct OT reference strings. Genesis 49:10 is
split into five entries; Isaiah 9:6 into eight. Publishing each fragment separately
preserves the advertised “351” count, but risks repetition and artificial inflation.

Each claim is a separately publishable, bite-size editorial and social-media unit.
Keep an internal relationship between claims from the same OT passage so research,
context, terminology, and conclusions remain consistent. Related claims may be
presented as an identified series, but each post must stand on its own.

## HQ-003: Whose Christian argument should be presented?

**Decision: accepted.**

Several seed descriptions are idiosyncratic and weaker than mainstream Christian
exegesis. A critique of only the inherited wording could become a straw man.

Describe the strongest recognisable version found in a
named Christian scholarly or apologetic source, while preserving the seed-list claim
as provenance.

Every completed critical argument will then be challenged by a different language
model under the independent process in [REVIEW_PROTOCOL.md](REVIEW_PROTOCOL.md).

## HQ-004: What does “why it isn’t” mean?

**Decision: accepted.**

There are two distinct conclusions:

- the passage did not predict Jesus in its original historical/literary context;
- a later Christian typological reading is illegitimate or uninteresting.

The first can be well supported even where the second is a matter of theology or
literary interpretation.

Assess predictive force, textual fit, and historical context
separately. Avoid claiming that disproving prediction automatically disproves every
possible typological reading.

## HQ-005: Which textual base and translations will the project use?

**Decision: accepted.**

Some claims depend on the Septuagint, a disputed Hebrew reading, or a particular
English rendering. Genesis 49:10 (“Shiloh”) is an early example; Isaiah 7:14 and
Psalm 22:16 will make the choice unavoidable.

There is also a licensing issue for displaying full verse text.

Store references independently of translations; compare the Masoretic Text and
Septuagint where material; use the public-domain Berean Standard Bible (BSB) as the
initial display translation; and identify translation-dependent claims explicitly.

The BSB is chosen for modern readability and unrestricted republication, not as an
academically neutral authority. Where its wording materially affects a claim, show an
academically oriented alternative and explain the underlying Hebrew, Greek, or textual
issue. Reconsider a licensed translation such as the NASB or NRSVue if the project
later has the audience or resources to justify it.
